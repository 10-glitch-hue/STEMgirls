The T is for Technology!

Technology is the practical application of scientific knowledge, utilizing systems, tools, and methods to solve issues and enhance human lives. It encompasses both the actual goods (hardware) and the intangible knowledge (software) and institutional frameworks (orgware) required to produce and utilize them, ranging from basic instruments like a hammer or wheel to sophisticated systems like computers and the internet.

According to the latest Nash Squared Digital Leadership Report, around 14% of global tech leaders in 2023 were women – the same percentage as in 2022. Shockingly, AIPRM finds that the proportion of women working in tech has remained stagnant, from 35% in 1984 to an estimated 32% in 2023.

Influential Women in Technology

From bluetooth to essential computer programs, women have always been at the forefront of invention in technology, even when society didn't allow.

Ada Lovelace
1815 - 1852

"That brain of mine is something more than merely mortal; as time will show."
Ada Lovelace, born in London, is often celebrated as the first computer programmer. Her mother, determined to nurture Ada’s analytical mind, insisted she study mathematics and science. In her groundbreaking notes on Charles Babbage’s proposed Analytical Engine, she envisioned how the machine could compute not just numbers but complex problems. This foresight laid the groundwork for modern computing. Her legacy is celebrated annually on Ada Lovelace Day, which highlights the contributions of women in STEM fields. 		Mary Allen Wilkes
1937 - Now

“I'll bet you don't have a computer in your living room.”
Mary Wilkes was born in Chicago and graduated from Wellesley College in 1959 with a degree in philosophy. She was told in the eighth grade by her geography teacher that she would be a computer programmer when she grew up. Mary helped develop the LINC (Laboratory Instrument Computer), regarded as the first personal computer. She even had one in her home—a groundbreaking concept at the time. As a programmer and technical writer, Mary paved the way for the development of user-friendly computing, making technology more accessible to the average person.
Annie Easley
1933 - 2011

"Don’t give up on it. Just stick with it. Don’t listen to people who always tell you it’s hard, and walk away from it."
Annie Easley attended Xavier University where she majored in pharmacy for around 2 years. She then began her career at NACA (later NASA) as one of only four African American employees at the time. She worked on code that contributed to energy-efficient batteries and space technology. Easley actively encouraged more women and African Americans to enter STEM fields, inspiring countless individuals to pursue tech careers.		 Radia Perlman
1951 - Now

"Music is very similar to a network in that there are all these different instruments playing their own parts, and somehow it fits together wonderfully."
Radia Perlman is a prominent American computer programmer and network engineer. In the 1970s, she created TORTIS, a programming language for children. She significantly advanced network efficiency in the 1980s by inventing the spanning tree algorithm and protocol, which transformed Ethernet networking capabilities. Perlman is recognized as a pivotal contributor to the development of the Internet, earning her the title "Mother of the Internet."
Hedy Lamarr
1914-2000

"Hope and curiosity about the future seemed better than guarantees. That's the way I was. The unknown was always so attractive to me... and still is."
Hedy Lamarr was an Austrain-American self-taught inventor and film actress, who was awarded a patent in 1942 for her "secret communication system", designed with the help of the composer George Antheil. This frequency hopping system was intended as a way to set radio-guided torpedos off course during the war, but the idea eventually inspired Wi-Fi, GPS and Bluetooth technology commonly used today. Unfortunately, as a natural beauty seen widely on the big screen in films like Samson and Delilah and White Cargo, society has long ignored her inventive genius. 		Elizabeth Feinler
1931 - Now

"I never had in my mind that I couldn't do something."
Elizabeth Jocelyn "Jake" Feinler is an American information scientist. From 1972 until 1989 she was director of the Network Information Systems Center at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI International). Her group operated the Network Information Center (NIC) for the ARPANET as it evolved into the Defense Data Network (DDN) and the Internet. Her work on transitioning to the Domain Name System (DNS) introduced the familiar “.com,” “.org,” and “.gov” protocols. Her contributions are foundational to the internet as we know it today.
