Learn more about young girls that made or are actively making an impact!

Gitanjali Rao - U.S.A

Gitanjali Rao, an 19-year-old inventor and aspiring scientist, is notable for her contribution to STEM education globally. She invented a lead contamination detection tool, for which she received the EPA Presidential award. Rao also created "Epione," for diagnosing opioid addiction, and "Kindly," an anti-cyberbullying service. Recognized as one of Forbes' "30 Under 30 in Science" and TIME's "Top Young Innovator," she has inspired over 85,000 students across 46 countries through her workshops. Additionally, she authored "Young Innovator’s Guide to STEM," available in 6 languages. Appointed UNICEF Youth Advocate in 2021, she addresses social issues through science and has received humanitarian awards for her work. Currently, she is studying at MIT and was a delegate to the 2019 Congress of Future Medical Leaders.

Margaret and Raina - Kenya

Asante Africa Youth Livelihood Program (YLP) student club leaders (first-year high school students) Margaret and Raina from Wamba Girls Secondary School in Kenya were concerned about dirty water waste in their community. After completing Asante Africa Foundation’s YLP program, the girls sought innovative ways to clean their community water. Through hard work, patience, trial and error, and testing scenarios, the girls invented a water purifier that also desalinates water. In 2022 these young innovators presented their water purifier to Kenya’s NTV TV station. Margaret and Raina won the Young Scientist Kenya Award 2022 for their water purifier invention and the girl’s innovation was recognized on Earth Day 2023 by Michelle Obama!

Anya Pogharian - Canada/Armenia

When Anya was given a high school science project, she was working at a hospital dialysis unit. She spent 300 hours at the age of 17 creating a new, affordable dialysis machine that would only cost $500 instead of the $30,000 models that are now available. Additionally, instead of forcing patients to travel to hospitals for four-hour treatments multiple times a week, it would function from home. She was motivated by the hospital's patients, who had trouble getting there and were exhausted from their treatments. In addition to receiving scholarships and honours, she was given a summer internship to finish testing her innovation before putting it on the market.

Emma Yang - U.S.A

At 16-year-old, Emma Yang became the founder of Timeless, a mobile app designed to help Alzheimer’s patients maintain engagement with loved ones. Recognized by influential figures like former US Attorney General Preet Bharara, Emma has received numerous accolades for her contributions, including the MIT-Solve Brain Health Challenge winner in 2017 and the Grand Prize Winner of the Women Startup Challenge in 2018. An accomplished coder and advocate for STEM, she has also excelled academically as a Davidson Young Scholar and a participant in prestigious programs like the Johns Hopkins University's SET. Emma is actively involved in public speaking at various international forums and writes for Quiet Revolution’s Quiet Diaries. Through her work, she aims to inspire others, particularly girls, to explore science and technology.

Elisa Torres Durney - Chile

Elisa Torres Durney is a Chilean social entrepreneur and STEM activist. She is the founder and executive director of Girls in Quantum. In 2023 she became one of the 10 best students in the world. She has reached 20k+ attendees and students by speaking at 50+ international conferences having been recognized by IBM, The Economist, United Nations, The Lancet, Women Economic Forum, Ernst & Young, TEDx, John Hopkins University, Quantum Basel Global Symposium, Mercado Libre, Swiss Cyber Security Days, MetLife Triangle TechX and schools in different countries. She has also been featured on national & international television for her work in making quantum computing accessible.

Calcea Johnson and Ne'Kiya Jackson - U.S.A

Calcea Johnson and Ne’Kiya Jackson, two students from New Orleans, made a stunning breakthrough while in high school by discovering five new trigonometric proofs of the Pythagorean Theorem — a theorem more than 2,000 years old, long thought impossible to prove via trigonometry without circular logic. They later found a general format that could potentially produce at least five additional proofs. They presented their work at a major conference of the American Mathematical Society and published in the journal The American Mathematical Monthly. Nonetheless, the girls denied seeing themselves as math geniuses and dismissed any interest in pursuing careers in mathematics. Calcea is now studying environmental engineering at Louisiana State University, while Ne’Kiya is pursuing a pharmacy degree at Xavier University of Louisiana.
